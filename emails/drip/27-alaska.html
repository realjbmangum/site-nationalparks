Denali, Glacier Bay, Katmai, Kenai Fjords — Alaska's parks are the wildest places in the national park system.

Alaska's 8 National Parks Are a Different Kind of Wild

Alaska has eight national parks, and they're nothing like the parks in the lower 48. These are places where grizzlies outnumber people, glaciers calve into the ocean, and the highest peak in North America rises 20,310 feet above sea level. If "remote" and "wild" are what you're after, Alaska delivers.

Denali is the most accessible Alaska park and a bucket list destination on its own. The park road stretches 92 miles into the wilderness, but private vehicles are only allowed for the first 15 miles. Take the park bus to Eielson Visitor Center (mile 66) for a clear-day view of Denali's summit. Wildlife is everywhere -- grizzlies, caribou, Dall sheep, moose, and wolves.

Kenai Fjords is your best bet for tidewater glaciers and marine wildlife. Take a boat tour out of Seward to see Exit Glacier calving ice into the ocean, plus humpback whales, sea otters, puffins, and orcas. The Harding Icefield Trail (8.2 miles round trip, strenuous) takes you up to a viewpoint over one of the largest icefields in the US.

Katmai is famous for one thing: brown bears catching salmon at Brooks Falls. The park webcam is one of the most-watched in the world, but seeing it in person is on another level. Peak bear activity is July and September. You'll need to fly in on a floatplane from King Salmon.

Glacier Bay is best experienced by boat or kayak. Cruise ships and day-tour boats take you past massive tidewater glaciers, and the kayaking here is world-class. Humpback whales feed in these waters all summer.

Alaska Planning Tip

The Alaska park season is short -- mid-June through mid-September for most parks. Book flights, buses, and lodging 6-12 months ahead, especially for Denali bus tickets and Katmai bear viewing permits. Bug spray is essential. Seriously, the mosquitoes are legendary.
